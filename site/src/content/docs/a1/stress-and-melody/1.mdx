**ка́ва. вода́. за́мок і замо́к.** — word stress in Ukrainian.

In the first three modules, you mastered all thirty-three letters of the Ukrainian alphabet and the apostrophe. You learned to distinguish consonants and vowels, decode hard and soft sounds, pronounce affricates, and read by syllables. Now we enter the heart of Ukrainian spoken melody. A written word on the page comes alive only when you know which syllable takes the vocal accent and how your voice flows across the sentence.

Today you begin mastering **на́голос** — word stress. In Ukrainian, word stress is dynamic, musical, and meaningful. It does not simply make a vowel louder; it shapes the identity of the entire word and, in many pairs of words, determines the meaning.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- Understand the two foundational properties of Ukrainian stress: it is **ві́льний** (free to fall on any syllable) and **рухо́мий** (mobile across inflected forms of a word);
- Identify and correctly pronounce meaning-distinguishing pairs (**за́мок** vs **замо́к**, **а́тлас** vs **атла́с**, **о́рган** vs **орга́н**, **сім'я́** vs **сі́м'я**);
- Maintain the cardinal rule of Ukrainian vowel purity: keep unstressed **[о]** clear and rounded (**молоко́**, **коро́ва**, **до́брого ра́нку**), with zero vowel reduction or "akanye";
- Use the standard linguistic synonyms **бу́ква** and **лі́тера** with confidence when analyzing Ukrainian orthography;
- Read descriptive sentences and natural dialogue with accurate stress and clear vocalic rhythm.

:::tip
In learning materials, dictionaries, and textbooks, word stress is marked with an acute accent (**ка́ва**, **вода́**, **молоко́**). In everyday Ukrainian books, newspapers, signs, and websites, the stress mark is not printed. You learn the stress as an organic, inseparable part of every new word.
:::

## Що таке́ на́голос: Ві́льний та рухо́мий

**На́голос** — stress — is the acoustic highlighting of one syllable in a word by giving it greater breath force, slightly higher pitch, and longer duration (Заболотний, 5 клас, с. 73). The vowel inside this syllable is called **наголо́шений голосни́й** — the stressed vowel. The other vowels in the word are **ненаголо́шені голосні́** — unstressed vowels.

Unlike languages with a fixed stress rule — such as French (where stress always falls on the final syllable), Polish (almost always on the penultimate syllable), or Czech (always on the first syllable) — Ukrainian stress has two defining characteristics:

1. **Ві́льний на́голос (Free stress):** Stress is not tied to any fixed position in the word. It can fall on:
   - **The first syllable:** **ма́ма** — mother, **та́то** — father, **ра́нок** — morning, **ка́ва** — coffee, **кни́га** — book;
   - **The middle syllable:** **столи́ця** — capital, **соба́ка** — dog, **маши́на** — car, **люди́на** — human / person;
   - **The final syllable:** **вода́** — water, **зима́** — winter, **рука́** — hand / arm, **метро́** — subway, **кафе́** — cafe.

2. **Рухо́мий на́голос (Mobile stress):** Stress can shift its position from one syllable to another when a word changes its grammatical form. For instance, the singular noun **голова́** (head) has final stress, but in the plural **го́лови** (heads), the stress leaps back to the first syllable. The word **рука́** (hand) becomes **ру́ки** (hands), and **село́** (village) becomes **се́ла** (villages).

When recording new vocabulary in your notebook, write both the spelling and the stress mark. Treating stress as an essential component of spelling ensures you build accurate mental pronunciation models from day one.

In Ukrainian grammar, both terms **бу́ква** and **лі́тера** are used as full synonyms for a written character. Each **бу́ква** or **лі́тера** in a printed word represents a sound, but it is the vocalic peak marked by **на́голос** that organizes the entire chain of letters into living speech.

| --- | --- | --- |
| **ма́ма**, **ка́ва**, **ра́нок** | пе́рший склад (first syllable) | **ма́-ма**, **ка́-ва**, **ра́-нок** |
| **столи́ця**, **соба́ка** | сере́дній склад (middle syllable) | **сто-ли́-ця**, **со-ба́-ка** |
| **вода́**, **зима́**, **метро́** | оста́нній склад (final syllable) | **во-да́**, **зи-ма́**, **мет-ро́** |

## Па́ри, що змі́нюють зна́чення: За́мок і замо́к

Because Ukrainian stress is free, shifting the stress to a different syllable can create an entirely different word. These pairs are called heteronyms or homographs. If you place the stress on the wrong vowel, you say a completely different word!

Consider these classic Ukrainian meaning-distinguishing pairs:

- **за́мок** (stress on the first syllable) — a fortress, a medieval palace or castle:
  *«На висо́кій горі́ стої́ть старови́нний за́мок — An ancient castle stands on the high hill.»*
- **замо́к** (stress on the final syllable) — a mechanical lock on a door or gate:
  *«Ось ключ, але́ залі́зний замо́к зла́маний — Here is the key, but the iron lock is broken.»*

- **а́тлас** (stress on the first syllable) — an atlas, a collection of geographical maps:
  *«У́чневі потрі́бен географі́чний а́тлас — The student needs a geographical atlas.»*
- **атла́с** (stress on the final syllable) — satin, a smooth, lustrous silk fabric:
  *«У ма́ми є су́кня з блиску́чого атла́су — Mother has a dress made of glossy satin.»*

- **о́рган** (stress on the first syllable) — an anatomical body organ (such as the heart, liver, or eye):
  *«Се́рце — це головни́й життє́вий о́рган — The heart is the primary vital organ.»*
- **орга́н** (stress on the final syllable) — a pipe organ, the grand musical instrument:
  *«У собо́рі щонеді́лі звучи́ть старови́нний орга́н — An ancient pipe organ sounds in the cathedral every Sunday.»*

- **сі́м'я** (stress on the first syllable) — seed or semen:
  *«Птахи́ шука́ють дрібне́ сі́м'я — The birds look for small seed.»*
- **сім'я́** (stress on the final syllable) — family:
  *«На́ша сім'я́ дру́жня та вели́ка — Our family is friendly and large.»*

:::note
**Important lexicographical note:** In older dictionaries, you might encounter the word pair `му́ка` (suffering) and `мука́` (flour). In modern standard Ukrainian, the word for flour is **бо́рошно** (**бі́ле бо́рошно** — white flour). The word **му́ка** in standard contemporary Ukrainian strictly designates suffering, torment, or agonizing labor. Avoid using `мука́` for cooking flour; always use **бо́рошно**.
:::

## Чи́стий ненаголо́шений звук [о]

One of the most defining musical features of the Ukrainian language is **vowel clarity**. In many neighboring languages (such as Russian or Belarusian), unstressed vowels weaken and blur. In particular, Russian reduces unstressed [о] to a lax [а] or neutral [ə] (a linguistic process called *akanye*).

In standard literary Ukrainian, **akanye does not exist**. Unstressed **о** is always pronounced as a clean, rounded, open **[о]**:

- **молоко́** [мо-ло-ко́] — milk. All three **о** sounds are pronounced clearly. The first two vowels are slightly shorter because they are unstressed, but they never turn into [а]. Pronouncing `малако` is an immediate phonetic error.
- **до́брого ра́нку** [до́б-ро-го ра́н-ку] — good morning. Both unstressed **о** letters in **до́брого** remain pure [о].
- **коро́ва** [ко-ро́-ва] — cow. Both **о** sounds stay pure [о].
- **соба́ка** [со-ба́-ка] — dog. The initial syllable is [со], never [са].
- **голова́** [го-ло-ва́] — head. Both initial syllables keep their distinct [о] quality.

Let us practice with syllable stepping. Tap your finger on the table for each syllable, keeping every vowel distinct:
- **мо — ло — ко́** (three clean [о] sounds)
- **до́б — ро — го** (three clean [о] sounds)
- **ко — ро́ — ва** (two clean [о] sounds)
- **го — ло — ва́** (two clean [о] sounds)

Remember that whether you call the written character **бу́ква** or **лі́тера**, the printed letter **О** always demands your lips to round softly and produce a true [о].
