# Your First Conversation

In our previous lessons, we explored how Ukrainian sounds and letters work together: the six foundational vowel sounds, the consonants that shape them, and the rhythm of Ukrainian stress (**на́голос**). Now it is time to put those sounds to work in your very first real-life conversations.

Before you speak, you will notice something special about Ukrainian: it is built to sound smooth, musical, and effortless. Ukrainian speakers actively avoid clumsy pile-ups of consonants. Once you understand this principle of sound harmony—called **милозву́чність**—you will step into natural exchanges: greeting someone at different times of day, asking how they are doing, sharing names, and parting politely.

---

## Euphony: У/В and І/Й

Ukrainian is celebrated for its melodic flow. In Ukrainian linguistics, this quality is known as **милозву́чність — euphony** (literally "sweet-soundingness"). The language dislikes heavy consonant tangles or awkward vowels colliding across word boundaries. To keep speech flowing smoothly, Ukrainian alternates between pairs of small words and prefixes.

At this stage, you do not need to memorize exhaustive lists of phonetic rules. Your goal is simply to train your ear to recognize two essential alternating pairs: **у / в** and **і / й**.

### The Alternating Pair: У and В

The preposition meaning "in" or "at" can appear as either **у** or **в**. They carry the exact same meaning—the choice depends entirely on the surrounding sounds:

- **Use в after a vowel** when the next word begins with a consonant:
  - **Ході́мо в кіно́! — Let's go to the cinema!** (The vowel **о** flows cleanly into **в**, followed by consonant **к**.)
  - **вона́ живе́ в Оде́сі — she lives in Odesa** (The vowel **е** links smoothly into **в**.)

- **Use у after a consonant**, or at the very start of a sentence before a consonant:
  - **у шко́лі — at school** (Starting with vowel **у** makes the consonant cluster **шк-** easy to pronounce.)
  - **У тебе́ га́рна кни́жка. — You have a nice book.** (Opening with **у** avoids starting with a cluster.)
  - **зайшо́в у дім — walked into the house** (Between consonants **в** and **д**, vowel **у** acts as a comfortable cushion.)

- **Special sound environments:** Before words starting with **в**, **ф**, or clusters like **льв-**, **св-**, **тв-**, Ukrainian always prefers **у**:
  - **у Льво́ві — in Lviv** (Saying **в Льво́ві** would force your lips to collide on two **v** sounds.)
  - **у тво́рі — in the essay** (Avoids three consonants in a row.)

This alternation even appears inside vocabulary pairs: **учи́тель / вчи́тель — teacher**, and **уже́ / вже — already**.

### The Alternating Pair: І and Й

The conjunction meaning "and" alternates in the same musical way between full vowel **і** and short glide **й**:

- **Use і between consonants:**
  - **брат і сестра́ — brother and sister** (Vowel **і** separates consonants **т** and **с**.)
  - **чай і ті́стечко — tea and pastry** (Vowel **і** cushions the flow between **й** and **т**.)

- **Use й after a vowel** when the next word begins with a vowel or single consonant:
  - **Марко́ й А́нна — Marko and Anna** (Glide **й** creates a gentle transition between vowels **о** and **а**.)

Listen to how naturally your voice moves when consonants and vowels alternate. Ukrainian creates this melody by design.

## Your First Conversation

When meeting people in Ukraine, greetings are spoken as complete, ready-to-use phrases. Which greeting you choose depends on the time of day and how well you know the person.

| Ukrainian | English | Context and usage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Приві́т!** | Hi! | Informal; with friends, classmates, peers |
| **До́брого ра́нку!** | Good morning! | Used in the morning until noon |
| **До́брий день!** | Hello! / Good day! | Neutral daytime greeting for any setting |
| **До́брий ве́чір!** | Good evening! | Used as daylight fades and evening begins |
| **Як спра́ви?** | How are you? | Friendly inquiry; literally "How are matters?" |
| **До́бре.** | Fine. / Well. | Simple, positive response |
| **Чудо́во.** | Great. / Wonderful. | Enthusiastic response |
| **Норма́льно.** | Okay. / Normal. | Balanced, neutral response |
| **А у тебе́?** | And you? / And with you? | Friendly reciprocal check after **Як спра́ви?** |
| **Як тебе́ зва́ти?** | What is your name? | Informal inquiry; literally "How to call you?" |
| **Мене́ зва́ти ...** | My name is ... | Stating your name; literally "Me they call..." |
| **А тебе́?** | And you? | Reciprocal question after a name exchange |
| **До поба́чення!** | Goodbye! | Standard, respectful farewell |
| **На все до́бре!** | All the best! | Warm parting formula |

Pay close attention to pronunciation in **До́брого ра́нку! — Good morning!**: both unstressed instances of **о** must remain pure, rounded **[о]** sounds. Never reduce them to English "uh" or Russian "a".

### Dialogue 1: Meeting a Classmate

Two classmates meet in the hallway before class. Listen to how they greet each other and check in:

> **А́нна:** Приві́т!  
> **Марко́:** Приві́т!  
> **А́нна:** Як спра́ви?  
> **Марко́:** До́бре. А у тебе́?  
> **А́нна:** Чудо́во.  

#### Dialogue Breakdown

| Ukrainian line | English breakdown | Pronunciation note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Приві́т!** | Hi! | Stress is on **-ві́т**; stretch your lips into a smile for **[і]**. |
| **Як спра́ви?** | How are you? | **спра́ви** has stress on **спра́-**; keep both syllables clean. |
| **До́бре. А у тебе́?** | Fine. And with you? | **До́бре** has stress on the first syllable; **у тебе́** has stress on **-бе́**. |
| **Чудо́во.** | Great. / Wonderful. | Stress is on the middle syllable: **чу-до́-во**. |

### Dialogue 2: Exchanging Names

Now Marko meets Sofia for the first time. They introduce themselves:

> **Марко́:** Приві́т! Як тебе́ зва́ти?  
> **Софі́я:** Приві́т! Мене́ зва́ти Софі́я. А тебе́?  
> **Марко́:** Мене́ зва́ти Марко́.  

#### Dialogue Breakdown

| Ukrainian line | English breakdown | Grammatical insight |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Як тебе́ зва́ти?** | What is your name? | Literally: "How is one to call you?" |
| **Мене́ зва́ти Софі́я.** | My name is Sofia. | Literally: "Me they call Sofia." |
| **А тебе́?** | And you? | Elliptical short form for **А тебе́ як зва́ти?** |
| **Мене́ зва́ти Марко́.** | My name is Marko. | Stress on **-ко́**: **Мар-ко́**. |

#### Crucial Etiquette Distinction: А у тебе́? vs А тебе́?

Notice how the follow-up question changes depending on what was asked:
- When someone asks **Як спра́ви? — How are you?**, the underlying idea is "How are things *with you*?" (**у тебе́**). Therefore, your reciprocal question is:  
  **А у тебе́? — And with you?**
- When someone asks **Як тебе́ зва́ти? — What is your name?**, the word **тебе́** is the direct target ("What do they call *you*?"). Therefore, your reciprocal question is:  
  **А тебе́? — And you?** (short for **А тебе́ як зва́ти?**). Never say **А у тебе́?** when exchanging names.

### Dialogue 3: Classroom Greeting

When entering a classroom or an office, you use the neutral daytime greeting:

> **Вчи́тель:** До́брий день!  
> **У́чні:** До́брий день!  
> **Вчи́тель:** Як спра́ви?  
> **У́чні:** До́бре!  

#### Dialogue Breakdown

| Ukrainian line | English breakdown | Register |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **До́брий день!** | Hello! / Good day! | Neutral/formal greeting; polite for teachers and strangers. |
| **Як спра́ви?** | How are you? | Friendly group check-in. |
| **До́бре!** | Fine! / Well! | Cheerful collective answer. |

### Gender in Greetings: Being Glad to See Someone

In Ukrainian, words that describe state or action often reflect grammatical gender. You will see this throughout the language, but here is your very first encounter:

| Phrase | English | Who speaks? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ра́дий тебе́ ба́чити.** | Glad to see you. | Spoken by a **male speaker** |
| **Ра́да тебе́ ба́чити.** | Glad to see you. | Spoken by a **female speaker** |

If a man says the phrase, he uses the masculine ending: **Ра́дий**. If a woman says the phrase, she uses the feminine ending: **Ра́да**. The person being addressed does not determine the ending—the gender of the **speaker** does.

### Sound Anatomy: Analyzing Приві́т

Let's conclude today's session by examining your very first greeting sound by sound. Look at how each letter builds the word **Приві́т**:

- **П** — voiceless stop consonant **[п]**
- **р** — voiced trill consonant **[р]**
- **и** — open, relaxed vowel **[и]**
- **в** — voiced approximant consonant **[в]**
- **і** — narrow, smiling vowel **[і]**
- **т** — voiceless dental stop consonant **[т]**

The word **Приві́т** contains exactly **two vowel sounds** (**[и]** and **[і]**) and **four consonant sounds**. Because every Ukrainian syllable requires a vowel sound, **Приві́т** has exactly two syllables: **При-ві́т**. The stress mark (**на́голос**) sits on the second syllable: **При-ві́т**.

### Recap and Next Step

You now possess everything you need for a complete first interaction in Ukrainian:
- Recognizing how **милозву́чність** selects **у / в** and **і / й** to keep sentences flowing smoothly.
- Selecting the right greeting for the moment: informal **Приві́т!**, early-morning **До́брого ра́нку!**, daytime **До́брий день!**, or evening **До́брий ве́чір!**.
- Asking **Як спра́ви?** and answering with **До́бре**, **Чудо́во**, or **Норма́льно**.
- Asking names with **Як тебе́ зва́ти?**, answering **Мене́ зва́ти...**, and reciprocating correctly with **А тебе́?**.
- Using **Ра́дий тебе́ ба́чити** (male) or **Ра́да тебе́ ба́чити** (female).
- Parting politely with **До поба́чення!** and **На все до́бре!**.
